# The Case for a Company-Wide Rollout of In-House AI

## Key message

In a six-week pilot (Nov 2 – Dec 11, 2026), the in-house AI cut document search time by **76%** and cut root-cause report drafting time by **61%**. There were **no security incidents**. All participants want to keep using it, and two more teams have already asked for access. The tool works, it is safe, and people want it.

## Reason 1: It saves a lot of time on core work

| Task | Before | After | Time saved | Reduction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Searching past quality documents | 25 min | 6 min | 19 min per search | 76% |
| Drafting a defect root-cause report | 3 h | 1 h 10 min | 1 h 50 min per report | 61% |

- Searches are about **4x faster**. A report now takes **less than 40%** of the time it used to.
- Both tasks come up again and again in quality work, so the savings add up. For example, someone who runs 10 searches a week saves over 3 hours a week on searching alone.

## Reason 2: The security risk was controlled

- The model ran on **company servers**, so no internal data left the company.
- Six weeks of real use on sensitive quality and defect documents produced **zero security incidents**.
- Data leakage is usually leadership's biggest worry about generative AI. Running the model in-house deals with that worry directly.

## Reason 3: The people who used it want to keep it

- **3 out of 3** participants want to keep using it.
- New tools often fail because people stop using them. Full voluntary uptake suggests that risk is low.

## Reason 4: Other teams are already asking for it

- The **design and planning teams** asked for access on their own.
- Rolling it out would meet demand that already exists, not push a tool on reluctant teams. It also suggests the benefits carry over to work outside quality.

## Recommended ask

Propose a **phased company-wide rollout** instead of switching everyone on at once:

1. **Phase 1:** Expand to the design and planning teams, since they have already asked.
2. **Phase 2:** Extend to the rest of the company, tracking the same metrics (time per task, user satisfaction, security incidents).

A phased plan is easier to approve. It also produces the larger dataset leadership will want before committing fully.

## Questions to prepare for

**"Three people is a small sample."**
Agree, and make that the reason for phasing. The pilot shows a clear signal, and Phase 1 tests it with more people and different kinds of work.

**"What does this save across the company? What's the ROI?"**
The pilot data shows time saved per task but not how often each task happens. Before the meeting, ask the quality team for rough counts and use this formula:

> Hours saved per year = (searches per person per week × 19 min × 48 weeks + reports per person per year × 110 min) × number of users

Also have the cost side ready: server and hardware costs, licensing (if any), and the staff time needed to run and support the system.

**"Was the output as good as before?"**
The figures show speed, not quality. If reviewers checked the AI-assisted reports, or the reports needed little rework, add a line saying so. If nobody tracked this, include it as a Phase 1 metric.

**"Can our servers handle more users?"**
Check with IT how much capacity the current setup has, and what it would cost to scale up to the design and planning teams and then company-wide.
