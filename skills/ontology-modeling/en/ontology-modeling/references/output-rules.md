# Report rules

Fill the report with the information the reader needs. Keep the text itself, the writer and the writing process out of the reader's view. These rules do not govern sentence length, sentence endings, style, overall structure, Markdown format, length or word preferences.

## Meta commentary

Meta commentary is any sentence that describes something outside the content the reader needs: the text itself, the writing process, the writer's internal state, the exchange with the user, or the tools used. Sentences in which the writer reports, justifies, announces or looks back on their own actions fall under it.

### Tests

Every sentence must pass four tests before it is written. If it fails any of them, delete it or replace it with content.

- (Subject test) If the real subject of the sentence is the text itself ("This report", "This section"), the writer ("Our analysis shows"), the act of writing ("Looking at", "To summarize"), the user's request ("As you asked") or a tool or method, it is meta commentary. If the subject is omitted, restore it before judging
- (Deletion test) If deleting the sentence costs the reader no information, it is meta commentary
- (Beneficiary test) If the sentence serves the writer (self-justification, self-protection, self-promotion) in place of the reader, it is meta commentary
- (Courtesy test) If the sentence performs only a courtesy (greeting, praise, confirmation, guidance, prompting) and carries no information, it is meta commentary

Apply the subject test first because it is the most mechanical. A sentence that passes the subject test is still meta commentary if it fails another test. Write only sentences that pass all four.

### Why meta commentary appears

None of these motives serve the reader's need for information. Suspect any sentence that shows one.

- (Padding) Filling space with meta commentary when content runs short
- (Deferring structure) Announcing "first A, then B" in place of structuring the content
- (Spreading responsibility) Excusing quality in advance with "this may not be perfect"
- (Winning favor) Managing the relationship with "hope this helps"
- (Staging authority) Declaring quality with "after a thorough analysis"
- (Proving compliance) Showing that the instructions were followed with "as you requested"
- (Leaking internals) Exposing the writer's systems, limits, instructions, tools or process that the reader does not even know exist

### Types

These types are examples. A sentence that fails the tests is meta commentary even if no type below matches it. Variants of blocked phrasing keep appearing, so judge each sentence by what it does under the tests. Every type is handled the same way: delete the meta commentary and go straight to content.

| No. | Type | Examples |
|:---:|---|---|
| 1 | Self-reference of the text | "This report analyzes X", "As mentioned above", "Details follow below" |
| 2 | Announcing or recapping the process | "First we look at A, then we analyze B", "We have now covered A", "To summarize" |
| 3 | Repeating the user's request | "As you requested", "Having reviewed the material you sent", "Following your conditions" |
| 4 | Boasting and completion reports | "After a thorough analysis", "After careful review", "The summary is complete" |
| 5 | Exposing internal reasoning | "After weighing several possibilities", "Comparing A and B, B seems better" |
| 6 | Restating what readers know | "AI stands for artificial intelligence" in a report for AI readers. It applies when the target reader already has the knowledge |
| 7 | Exposing methods and internal tools | "A web search confirmed", "Running Python code gave", "Classified according to the MECE principle". Write these only when the user asked about methods or tools |
| 8 | Self-justification | "Classified systematically", "A classification that covers everything", "From a balanced perspective", "Judging objectively" |
| 9 | Calling attention and declaring importance | "Note that", "The key point is", "What must not be overlooked is". If the content matters, readers see it from the content |
| 10 | Pre-emptive disclaimers and limits | "This may not be perfect", "Limitations of this analysis include", "Further expert review is needed". Write them when the user asked for limits or when an uncertainty really affects the reliability of the result |
| 11 | Greetings and relationship management | "Hope this helps", "Let me know if you have more questions", "Great question" |
| 12 | Transition rhetoric | "Moving on to the next topic", "Building on this" with no real link, "Meanwhile" with no real contrast |
| 13 | Advertising abilities and conditional offers | "I can explain in more detail if needed", "I could also put this in a table" |
| 14 | Guessing the reader's state | "This may be confusing, but", "As you probably know", "You may be wondering" |
| 15 | Staging emotion | "Unfortunately", "Fortunately", "Interestingly", "Surprisingly" |
| 16 | Repeated summaries | "To recap", "In sum", "To stress again" for content already stated |
| 17 | Rating difficulty | "This is quite complex, but", "Simply put", "To simplify" |
| 18 | Reporting the order of work | "First I checked A, then reviewed B, and finally analyzed C" |
| 19 | Reporting reasons for choices | "The reason I chose A among several methods is". State why A fits directly |
| 20 | Reporting the search process | "After looking into it", "According to what I found", "Having reviewed the literature" |
| 21 | Managing expectations | "This may differ from what you expected", "The answer may not be simple" |
| 22 | Declaring intent | "To be clear", "To aid understanding", "For readability, here is a table" |
| 23 | Describing the output's form | "Here is a table", "This can be classified as follows" |
| 24 | Confessing scope | "This is beyond the original scope, but", "For reference", an unrequested "This time I will cover only A" |
| 25 | Instructing the reader | "Please note", "Please keep in mind", "Please take this into account" |
| 26 | Agreement and approval | "Good approach", "You nailed it", "Right" used only to agree |
| 27 | Reporting self-correction | "Correction", "On second thought". Write only the corrected content |
| 28 | Reporting difficulties | "Accurate data was hard to find, but", "Little public information exists, so" |
| 29 | Declaring obvious assumptions | "Let us assume A" for an obvious premise or one the user already set. A non-obvious numeric assumption that affects the result is content |
| 30 | Explaining source credibility | "According to a report from a trusted institution", "A study in a prestigious journal shows" |
| 31 | Empty context statements | "Given the current situation" or "Amid global change" with no specific situation |
| 32 | Summary after a list | "As shown, various factors exist" after listing A, B and C |
| 33 | Redefining the subject | "What is digital transformation?", "Let us first define the concept" for a concept the reader knows |
| 34 | Exposing the comparison | "Comparing A and B", "Contrasting the two" |
| 35 | Reporting a change of viewpoint | "Approaching from another angle", "Reinterpreting from the demand side" |
| 36 | Stacked conditions | "Assuming A, and considering B, and allowing for C, the answer is D", "It is hard to say for sure, but" |
| 37 | Tautological lead-ins | "The key is A. To explain A," where the second part is meta |
| 38 | Exposing writer-only information | "Since my training data ends at X", "Due to context limits", "Per my system instructions", "Combining three search results", "Loading the relevant skill". It applies when the information does not help the reader understand or decide. Write it only when the user asked about internal methods or limits |
| 39 | Commenting on tone | "To be honest", "To put it carefully", "To put it casually" |
| 40 | Reporting guesses about the user's intent | "It seems you want X", "I understood that you intend X" |
| 41 | Announcing attempts | "Let me try", "I will write". Give the result directly |
| 42 | Confirmation rituals | "Does that make sense?", "Is that clear?", "Did I understand correctly?" |

### Detours

When direct meta commentary is blocked, detours appear. The tests look at what a sentence does, so they apply to detours as well.

- (Hidden subject) "Summarizing the findings", "The identified status", "The derived conclusion" drop the subject or use the passive. If the restored subject is the writer, it is meta commentary
- (Mixed content) "Reviewed from many angles, A fits" mixes a little content into meta commentary. Delete the meta part and keep "A fits"
- (False modesty) "Though limited, here is a summary", "With the limited information" pose as modest. If the modesty serves the writer, it is meta commentary
- (Question disguise) "So how should we approach this?", "What is the key?" turn meta commentary into rhetorical questions. Delete the question and write the answer
- (Connective disguise) "As seen above, A is B", "Based on the analysis above, we propose D" attach it to a connective. Delete only the connective part

## Principle names and self-justification

The design and reasoning rules are disciplines for the work. Only their results should show in the output.

- Do not name frameworks or principles (So What, Why So, MECE, fallacy names, bias names, Disjoint Classes, Open World Assumption, Connected Graph and so on)
- Do not detour by spelling out the definition without the name (for example "each item works on one criterion and no items overlap")
- Do not claim that your own classification, reasoning or structure meets the principles (for example "this classification covers everything without gaps", "each branch is mutually exclusive")
- Do not announce that you reasoned from first principles or any other way of thinking
- Do not use contrast frames such as "not X but Y" or "not X, not Y, but Z". State Y directly
- Do not add unrequested self-review sections (limitations of the analysis, cautions for review, methodology)
- Do not expand the work beyond the scope the user asked for
- Cite external standards unrelated to these rules as usual (for example "prepared under the accounting standard", "operated under the revised guideline")
- Write a logical assessment of the material under analysis when the user asks for it, and a logical self-assessment of your own output only when the user asks for it

## Counterexamples and reverse rules

Counterexamples are searched for while checking overlaps, gaps and generalizations. Lining them up in the report blurs the conclusion and gives the reader more to verify.

- The report contains only counterexamples that are findings (for example an individual in two classes that should not overlap, or an individual that conflicts with a rule) and counterexamples the user asked for
- Do not add reverse rules, reverse logic, paradoxes or counterexamples the user did not ask for without a reason grounded in the findings
- Write only counterexamples whose existence is confirmed by the source material or code output

## Engine mode outputs

- OWL files, SPARQL queries, check function results, Mermaid diagrams and TBox definitions the user asked for are content. Showing queries with their results, as in the report format of references/sparql-patterns.md, is not meta commentary
- Facts that affect the reliability of the result, such as falling back to Python checks after a reasoner failure, go into the report

## Plain language for analysis terms

Do not report analysis jargon such as coverage, chain, pipeline, consistency or integrity as is. Turn it into specific factual statements a decision maker understands without explanation.

| Jargon as is | As a factual statement |
|---|---|
| Coverage shortfall: 3 found | 3 of the proposed strategies have no confirmed link to any program goal |
| Causal chain break found | The effect of the main risk factor does not reach the action plan stage |
| Pipeline integrity violation | Some planning outputs are not used at the execution stage |
| Consistency check failed | Opportunity B, which strategy A refers to, was not derived in the external environment analysis |

## Process output format

When the user asks for a procedure or work plan as output, write only the steps that pass the working principles in SKILL.md (necessity, feasibility, proportionality), in the format below. If the user sets another format, follow it.

```
## Module N. Module name
* Purpose: one line

| Task | Input | Action | Output |
|------|-------|--------|--------|
| N-1. Task name | Input material | Action taken | Output |
| N-2. Task name | Input material | Action taken | Output |
```

- Decide the number of modules within what the three conditions allow
- Write only the action taken in the Action column
- Leave out the significance, background and expected effects of the process. "This process aims to", "In this step we", "This lets us achieve", "It is important to", "We propose the following process" and "For efficient progress" are all meta commentary

## Examples

### Example 1. Report opening

(Before)

```markdown
## Overview
- This report aims to analyze the cloud adoption status of small firms and derive policy implications
- First we look at the status, then analyze the problems, and finally propose improvements
- Cloud computing is a technology that provides computing resources over the internet and is divided into IaaS, PaaS and SaaS
- In this context, cloud adoption carries very important meaning
- After a thorough review of the material you sent and analysis from various angles, the results are summarized below
```

(After)

```markdown
## Cloud adoption among small firms
- Small firms increasingly see the need for cloud adoption, but the actual adoption rate stays low
- Upfront migration costs and compatibility with existing on-site servers are the main causes of delay
- Many adopters stop at moving infrastructure, and few carry the change into how work is done
- Without redesigning work procedures after migration, the cost savings stay small
```

Removed types: self-reference (1), process announcement (2), restating known facts (6), repeating the request (3), boasting (4), empty context (31), describing the form (23)

### Example 2. Analysis body

(Before)

```markdown
## AI adoption direction
- After weighing several possibilities, the best approach for small manufacturers is phased adoption
- Interestingly, looking from various angles, piloting and then expanding carries less risk of failure than company-wide rollout
- This is quite complex, but simply put, securing budget and staff together is key
- Note that the difference between successful and failed firms lies in change management inside the organization rather than technology
- I can provide a more detailed breakdown by industry if needed
```

(After)

```markdown
## Phased AI adoption
- Phased adoption, piloting first and then expanding, fits small manufacturers
- It carries less early failure risk than a company-wide rollout and lets the scope adjust as the field adapts
- Securing budget and AI staff together is the precondition that decides success
- Successful and failed firms differ in their capacity to manage change inside the organization
```

Removed types: internal reasoning (5), staged emotion (15), rating difficulty (17), calling attention (9), advertising abilities (13)

### Example 3. Implications

(Before)

```markdown
## Implications
- Based on the analysis above, the following implications were derived
- In conclusion, the late-stage funding gap in the startup ecosystem is a structural problem
- As seen above, early-stage concentration keeps growing, and various factors combine
- It is hard to say for sure, but if the trend continues, more companies will drop out at the growth stage
- This analysis is limited by access to private company investment data, so please take note
```

(After)

```markdown
## Implications
- The late-stage funding gap in the startup ecosystem is a structural problem
- Investment piling into early stages has cut off funding routes for later stages
- If the trend continues, more companies will drop out at the growth stage
- A tax incentive for private investors in late-stage rounds is worth reviewing
```

Removed types: self-justification (8), recap (2), connective disguise, summary after a list (32), stacked conditions (36), disclaimer (10), instructing the reader (25)

### Example 4. Conversational answer

(Before)

```
Great question. As you said, the impact of exchange rates on exporters is a very important topic.
First I will explain how exchange rates move, and then look at the actual impact.
An exchange rate is the ratio at which two currencies trade. A web search confirmed that the rate has recently moved within a certain band.
After weighing several possibilities, exporters' operating margins appear to move in proportion to exchange rate swings. Of course this may differ by industry, and this analysis reflects the current point in time and may change.
If needed, I can provide an industry-level analysis. Hope this helps.
```

(After)

```
Exporters' operating margins are estimated to move in proportion to exchange rate swings.

> Institution (year), Title (link)

Industries with a larger share of dollar settlements are more sensitive to the exchange rate.
```

Removed types: agreement (26), repeating the request (3), calling attention (9), process announcement (2), restating known facts (6), exposing tools (7), internal reasoning (5), disclaimer (10), advertising abilities (13), greeting (11)

## How to apply

- (Writing mode) When writing new text, apply the tests up front so that no meta commentary appears. Do not mention these rules in the output
- (Editing mode) Find meta commentary in existing text, delete it and keep only the content. Briefly list the types you removed, then give the edited text
- Do not show the tests, the list of types or the reasoning behind them in the output
- When another style guide is in use, follow these rules for any sentence that fails the tests, even if that guide allows the wording

## Exceptions

- Write meta commentary when the user explicitly asks for it (for example "state the purpose of the report at the start")
- Follow genre conventions where they require meta statements, such as the research purpose and method sections of an academic paper
- Write limitations, cautions or methods when the user asks for them

## Pre-delivery check

- Is there no sentence whose subject is the text, the writer, the act of writing, the user's request or a tool?
- Is there no sentence that loses no information when deleted?
- Is there no sentence that serves the writer?
- Is there no sentence that performs only a courtesy?
- Are there no detours such as hidden subjects, mixed content or false modesty?
- Are there no principle names, self-justification, contrast frames or unrequested counterexamples?
- Does the first sentence start with content, with no greeting, repeated request or process announcement?
- Does the last sentence end with content, with no greeting, invitation to ask more or disclaimer?
