# One message per unit

Put one message in each slide, chart, diagram, paragraph, and table so the reader knows where to look. Apply this to slides and visuals first, and the same way to paragraphs of text. This file does not govern style, vocabulary, how a table of contents is organized, Markdown hierarchy, or sentence length.

## Problems to solve

- (Department-store listing) Laying out related information at equal weight with no message. The burden of choosing and judging passes from the writer to the reader, who cannot tell where to look.
- (Message overload) Two or more independent claims packed into one slide or paragraph, so it is unclear what is being said.
- Both problems share a cause: the habit of placing all available information without choosing what to deliver.

## Units and messages

- A unit is a complete block the reader sees and digests at once. One slide, one diagram, infographic, or chart, one paragraph, and one table are each a unit.
- A message is the one judgment sentence the reader should remember from the unit. A judgment sentence is a complete claim with a subject and a predicate (for example "Low-cost imports are eroding domestic market profitability").
- Topic labels such as "Market status", "Background", and "Key issues" cannot serve as messages.
- Test with "What is the one sentence a reader of this unit should remember?" If there is no answer, the message is missing. If the answer strings together two or more independent claims, the unit is overloaded.
- Each unit delivers exactly one message. If there are two or more, add a slide, split the paragraph, or split the chart.
- Put the message at the head of the unit. The headline sentence carries it on a slide, the title or caption on a chart, and the first sentence in a paragraph. Chart title placement follows visual.md.
- In text with headers, use the header and the first sentence together to deliver the message. A sentence-style header carries the message itself, and the first sentence moves into the evidence. A keyword-style header announces what the message is about, and the first sentence carries the judgment.
- If what the header announces and the message of the first sentence differ, rename the header or move the content.

## Choosing elements

- By default, keep the core elements that support the message to 3-4 or fewer per unit.
- The chosen elements do not overlap and each covers a different part of the message.
- Do not fit the count mechanically. Do not stretch content that two elements cover to three or four. Frameworks with a built-in count, such as a four-quadrant matrix or a five-step process, keep their count.
- If five or more seem necessary, handle them in this order. First check whether messages are mixed, and split the unit if there are two or more. Next group the items into 3-4 higher categories and move the details down a level. Finally delete items that do not support the message.
- Compress by choosing elements. Keeping every element and only shortening the sentences leaves the same number of elements, so density rises and the cognitive load stays.
- Drop the elements you did not choose, and write the ones you keep in enough depth.
- Delete in this order: information unrelated to the message, background the reader already knows, duplicate evidence that supports the same conclusion again, and decorative numbers and cases that do not help judgment.
- By default, delete content that was not chosen. Do not keep it in the body because it took effort to write.
- If deleting is hard, only two paths are allowed. Split it into its own unit if it has independent message value, or move it to an appendix, footnote, or attachment if it has only reference value.
- Do not take the halfway path of mentioning something in the body while leaving out its details.

## Showing hierarchy

- Inside a unit, give different weight to three layers: the message, the supporting elements, and the details.
- Fix one point of emphasis per unit.
- On slides and designs, draw the eye with size, position, and color, and treat the rest with smaller size and lower saturation.
- In charts, bring out one emphasized series and treat comparison series with lower saturation. Which data points get labels follows chart.md.
- If every element has the same weight, there is no point of emphasis, so rearrange.

## Signs of department-store listing

If any of these signs appears, restructure.

- Five or more parallel items sit at the same level.
- A paragraph has two or more sentences with independent claims.
- The title or headline stops at a topic label and does not become a judgment sentence.
- Every item has the same visual weight and amount of text, so there is no point of emphasis.
- Elements of different kinds, such as cause and effect, phenomenon and response, or fact and opinion, are mixed in one list.
- What the header announces differs from the message of the first sentence.
- You cannot answer "the one sentence to remember from this unit".

## Working method

- (New writing) Break the output into a list of units. Fix each unit's message as one judgment sentence. Choose 3-4 or fewer supporting elements, and delete, split, or move the rest. Lay out the hierarchy of message, supporting elements, and details. Check the listing signs, then deliver. Do not reveal the name of this procedure or the checking process in the output.
- (Revising existing output) Extract every message per unit and diagnose the count. Split units with too many messages and cut elements down to the criteria. Deliver the revision with a short diagnosis per unit.

## Examples

Slide (before): Under the title "Market status", eight items are listed at equal weight: domestic market size of 5 trillion won, 3.2% annual growth, competition among 7 companies, rising low-cost imports from China, a regulatory sandbox, the need to improve consumer awareness, a complex distribution structure, and slow adoption of new technology.

Slide (after): Set the headline to "Low-cost imports and many-player competition are eroding domestic market profitability". Keep only three supporting elements: seven companies competing in a 5 trillion won market increases pressure to cut prices, low-cost imports from China lowered the average selling price, and 3.2% annual growth is not enough to offset the fall in profitability. Move the regulatory sandbox to the next slide as an opportunity message, and delete the rest or move it to an attachment.

Paragraph (before): "The cause of the delayed product launch is unstable parts supply. The supplier's production capacity is also insufficient. Meanwhile, a competitor launched a similar product first and spends twice our marketing budget. Therefore we must move the launch date up, increase the marketing budget, and diversify suppliers." Three independent messages, the cause, the competitive situation, and the response, sit in one paragraph, so the first sentence does not represent the paragraph.

Paragraph (after): Split into three paragraphs, one per message. The first starts with "The cause of the delayed launch is unstable parts supply" and holds only the evidence that key parts come from a single supplier whose capacity falls short of our orders. The second starts with "While the delay continues, the competitive position is getting worse" and holds the competitor's earlier launch and doubled marketing budget. The third starts with "The first priority of the response is diversifying suppliers" and holds the reason that faster schedules and larger marketing budgets only pay off once parts supply is stable.

Chart (before): Titled "Results by division", with 12 bars in the same color and labels on every bar.

Chart (after): Set the message to "The materials division generated more than half of company-wide revenue growth". Paint only the materials bar in the accent color, keep only the top three others at low saturation, and combine the rest into "Other".

## Checklist

- Does every unit deliver only one message?
- Is the message a judgment sentence?
- Are there 3-4 or fewer core elements per unit (frameworks with a fixed count excepted)?
- Did you compress by choosing elements?
- Is there one point of emphasis per unit, and does the eye go there?
- Did you delete, split, or move the content you did not choose?
- Are elements of different kinds kept out of the same list?
- Do the header and the first sentence point to the same object?

## Exceptions

- Where the user explicitly asks for a full list or a detailed catalog, the count criteria do not apply to that part.
- For outputs whose purpose is completeness, such as laws, regulations, and checklists, apply only the hierarchy rules and not the count criteria.
- Frameworks and standard forms with a fixed count keep their count.
- Quotations and reproduced source text are exempt.
