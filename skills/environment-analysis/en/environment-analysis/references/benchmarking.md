# Benchmarking

The benchmarking module records cases of comparable subjects as facts and compares them with the subject on the same basis. The method started in practice at Xerox and was formalized by Camp (1989). It uses the performance and practices of comparison targets as a reference point to locate the subject. It does not write plans to copy a case or actions the subject should take. Later stages handle those judgments.

## Choosing targets

| Type | Comparison target |
|---|---|
| Internal | Other departments, sites, or businesses within the same organization |
| Competitive | Direct competitors in the same market |
| Functional | Organizations in other industries that perform the same function |
| Generic | Organizations that excel at a specific practice regardless of industry |

Common targets by subject type: competing and leading organizations for an organization, overseas cases and other regions for a region, and leading countries and companies for an industry and technology area.

- State the reason for choosing each target. The criteria are similarity in size, industry, and institutional setting, the availability of performance data, and access to material
- Record similarities and differences with the subject separately. When institutions, market size, or funding structure differ widely, record the difference in the conditions column of the case table
- Do not pick success stories alone. If cases with visible limits or discontinued cases are confirmed, include them too. Looking only at successes overstates success factors
- The default lookback period is the last 5 years

## Recording case facts

| Target | Country or region | Period | Implementing body | Content | Results | Limits and failure factors | Conditions | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

- Record results with the indicators and figures the source reports
- Record limits and failure factors only as the source states them, and mark them unconfirmed otherwise
- Record each case in the fact register with a BMK- ID

## Critical success factors

Rockart (1979) defined critical success factors as the limited number of areas in which satisfactory results ensure successful performance. They are ratings drawn from case facts, so keep them apart from the facts.

- Choose factors that appear across several cases first
- Cite the BMK- IDs of the cases behind each factor
- Mark a factor found in only one case as specific to that case
- Organize limiting factors the same way as success factors

| Factor | Type (success, limit) | Supporting case IDs | Shared across cases |
|---|---|---|---|

## Gap comparison

Put the subject and each comparison target side by side on the same indicators.

- Comparison requires the same indicator definition, reference year, unit, and currency. If they differ, skip the comparison or state the difference
- Compute conversions and gaps in code. Record the reference date and source of the exchange rate used
- Take the subject's figures from the internal status module or provided material, and cite the matching INT- IDs

| Indicator | Subject | Comparison target | Gap | Reference date | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

## Writing principles

- Keep case facts and critical success factor ratings apart
- Favor cases that connect to findings in earlier modules. Make it visible in the implications section which earlier facts each case factor corresponds to
- Do not write investment feasibility, adoption recommendations, or application plans

## Check

- Each target has a reason for selection, similarities, and differences
- Cases with limits or failure factors were considered
- Each critical success factor cites supporting case IDs
- Gap comparisons use the same indicator definition, reference year, and unit
- No recommendations or application plans are mixed in
