# Argument checks

> Translated from the Korean original. See the translation notice in SKILL.md.

Apply the principles in this file while you think. The output should read as ordinary text in the format the user asked for, and the logic checks should stay out of the body.

## Thinking principles

- Doubt and verify every claim before answering, including your own knowledge and the user's request. Doubt must lead to a productive conclusion.
- Ask why, what, how, and what the definition, criteria, and scope are. Set definitions, criteria, and scope at the most basic unit and do not mix them anywhere in the text. Avoid empty wordplay.
- Do not take common sense, authority, existing knowledge, marketing terms, famous frameworks, or trends for granted.
- Trace a problem to its root cause before proposing improvements. Do not stop at surface evidence. Repeat "why" and present the root grounds.
- Look past the literal wording and infer the user's intent. Actively try induction, drawing common conclusions from individual facts and cases.
- Conclusions and reasoning must be valid and clear enough for a third party to accept.
- Do not mix facts and judgments in one statement, one classification, or one line of reasoning.
- State definitions, scope, and criteria so a third party can follow the same process and reach the same conclusion.

## Paired claims and evidence

- Every claim needs a conclusion paired with evidence. Do not list evidence without a conclusion, and do not declare a conclusion without evidence.
- As soon as you state a conclusion, state the causal or statistical link that supports it.
- A third party must be able to verify the causal or implicational link between claim and evidence.
- When a conclusion does not follow logically from the evidence, fix the conclusion or strengthen the evidence.
- A conclusion that exceeds the scope of its evidence is an overgeneralization. Narrow the conclusion to the scope of the evidence.
- When the conclusion becomes too narrow to support a decision, gather more evidence.
- Tracing must work both ways, from conclusion to evidence and from evidence to conclusion. Tracing back from the conclusion must reach the same evidence.
- A speaker's background does not decide whether a statement is true. Preserve the original context when you quote.
- The burden of proof lies with the side making the claim. Do not use the lack of disproof as evidence.
- Formal validity and the truth of premises are separate. A valid form with a false premise yields an untrustworthy conclusion.

## Hypothesis thinking

- Form a hypothesis before gathering information, and select the information needed to test it.
- A hypothesis must be falsifiable. A hypothesis no outcome can refute is useless.
- An initial hypothesis is provisional. Revise it as soon as you find contrary information.

## Reality check

Do not use reasoning that borrows a form without checking it against reality, or defensive and mechanical thinking. This includes:

- Number designs that ignore real demand and markets
- Reasoning that bends facts or adjusts figures to fit a goal
- Defense designs that rely only on block lists to prevent risk
- Explanations built on theories that cannot be falsified
- Rosy forecasts without grounds
- Plans that copy only the form of a leading case without examining differences in conditions
- Classification schemes that mix facts and judgments
- A view that chases cost figures alone and an exaggerated marketing view

## Formal fallacies

- (Denying the antecedent) From "if A then B", do not infer "not B" from "not A". B can arise from other causes.
- (Affirming the consequent) From "if A then B", do not infer A from B.
- (Only the contrapositive holds) Only the contrapositive "if not B then not A" is equivalent to "if A then B". Verify the converse and the inverse separately.
- (Affirming a disjunct) From "A or B", do not infer "not B" from A. Distinguish inclusive from exclusive disjunction.
- (Undistributed middle) Do not infer "Z is X" from "X is Y" and "Z is Y".
- (Four terms) The middle term of a syllogism must carry the same meaning in both premises. With two meanings, the syllogism has four terms and the inference fails.
- (Modal confusion) Verify separately any inference over propositions involving possibility, necessity, or obligation.

## Informal fallacies

### Appeals to the speaker and circumstances

- (Appeal to authority) The fact that an authority or authoritative institution said something does not prove the claim. Before quoting, confirm the source has expertise in that field.
- (False authority) Do not use statements by sources whose field of authority differs from the topic.
- (Anonymous authority) Do not cite unnamed authority such as "experts say" or "a study shows".
- (Biased authority) Do not cite an authority with a stake in a position as neutral. Disclose the conflict of interest before quoting.
- (Ad hominem) Do not judge a statement by the speaker's traits, background, or past record.
- (Tu quoque) Do not dismiss a present claim because the speaker once acted similarly.
- (Circumstantial ad hominem) Do not judge a claim by the speaker's affiliation, origin, education, or political camp.
- (Guilt by association) Do not evaluate a person's claim by the actions of family, colleagues, or groups.
- (Appeal to pity) Sympathy is not a ground for a conclusion.
- (Appeal to force) Do not push a claim through threats, coercion, or power.
- (Appeal to wealth) Price, budget size, or capital do not guarantee a claim.
- (Appeal to the crowd) Majority agreement does not decide truth.
- (Appeal to moderation) The midpoint between two positions is not automatically correct.
- (Appeal to ridicule) Making an opposing claim look silly does not refute it.
- (Tone policing) A speaker's manner or attitude does not decide whether the content is valid.
- (Genetic fallacy) Do not judge a claim by its origin alone. Allow for change over time.

### Distortions of meaning

- (Equivocation) Do not use one word in two or more senses within one argument.
- (Ambiguity) Remove expressions with unclear referents and resolve grammatical ambiguity.
- (Use and mention confusion) Distinguish the word itself from the thing it refers to in notation.
- (Covert redefinition) When you use a word in a sense other than its common one, state the definition first.
- (Accent fallacy) Do not distort the original meaning by stressing part of a statement.
- (Straw man) Do not weaken or distort an opposing claim and then refute it. Quote the original claim and then refute it.
- (Quoting out of context) Preserve the original context when you quote. Do not distort meaning through partial excerpts.
- (Existence by definition) Naming something does not make it real.
- (Identity inference error) Distinguish metaphorical statements from propositional ones. Do not treat figurative expressions as factual statements.
- (Figurative misreading) Do not read rhetorical expressions literally or literal expressions rhetorically. Identify the level of the expression first.
- (Emotional loading) Do not attach excessive emotional modifiers to factual statements to sway judgment.

### Causal and structural fallacies

- (False cause) Do not assert causation from temporal sequence. "Y happened after X" does not mean "X caused Y".
- (Ignoring a common cause) When X and Y occur together, check for a third common cause Z.
- (Reversed causation) Before asserting that X causes Y from a correlation, check whether Y causes X.
- (Oversimplification) Do not reduce a result with several causes to one cause.
- (False analogy) Do not infer similarity in other attributes from partial similarity between two things. Check the essential differences first.
- (False equivalence) Do not treat two things as the same on the basis of superficial commonalities.
- (Gambler's fallacy) Past outcomes of independent trials do not affect future probabilities.
- (Conjunction fallacy) The probability of two events occurring together cannot exceed the probability of either event alone.
- (Base rate neglect) Consider the base rate of the population when you judge how representative a case is.
- (Confusing ends and means) Do not reverse the order of cause and effect or of means and end.
- (Exaggerated intent) Do not inflate the intent behind someone else's action at will.

### Generalization and category fallacies

- (Hasty generalization) Do not generalize to the whole from too few or unrepresentative cases.
- (Myopic induction) Even with enough cases, do not generalize when the sampling path is biased. Verify representativeness separately.
- (Hidden qualifier) Do not use partial qualifiers such as "almost", "most", or "many" while applying the conclusion to all.
- (Accident fallacy) Do not apply a general rule mechanically to a special situation. Check the exception conditions.
- (Principle confusion) Do not apply principles meant for different situations to one situation at once.
- (Fallacy of composition) Do not extend the properties of parts to the whole.
- (Fallacy of division) Do not reduce the properties of the whole to its parts.
- (Peak fallacy) Do not judge the group average by its top members.
- (No true Scotsman) Do not dismiss a counterexample as "not a true X". When a counterexample appears, revise the generalization itself.
- (Distinction without a difference) Do not distinguish essentially identical meanings through different wording.
- (Category error) Do not treat concepts from different categories as one category.

### Fallacies of topic and dialogue

- (Red herring) Do not switch to a topic unrelated to the one under discussion.
- (Irrelevant conclusion) Do not draw a conclusion different from the one the premises support.
- (Cutting the knot) Do not remove the issue itself instead of solving the problem.
- (Poisoning the well) Do not use expressions that block counterarguments in advance. Frame claims so they can be falsified.
- (Argument from ignorance) Do not deny something because it is unproven, or affirm something because it is unrefuted.
- (Personal incredulity) Finding something hard to understand does not prove it false.
- (Slippery slope) When you claim a small change must lead to an extreme outcome, verify the likelihood of each intermediate step.
- (False dilemma) When a situation does not split into two options, look for a third and a fourth.
- (Black-and-white thinking) Do not simplify a topic without objective criteria into two camps.
- (Perfect solution fallacy) Do not reject a workable solution because of partial flaws.
- (Loaded question) Do not ask questions with built-in premises. Separate the premise from the question.
- (Special pleading) Keep one standard. Do not grant exceptions to particular cases without justification.
- (Argument by repetition) Do not wear the other side down by repeating the same question.

### Fallacies of value and fact

- (Naturalistic fallacy) Do not derive an "ought" statement directly from an "is" statement.
- (Moralistic fallacy) Do not derive an "is" statement directly from an "ought" statement.
- (Appeal to novelty) Do not claim superiority because something is new.
- (Appeal to tradition) Do not claim legitimacy because something is old.
- (Wishful thinking) Do not assert something as fact because it is desirable.
- (Counterfactual premise) When you reason from a premise contrary to fact, limit the conclusion to the range where the premise is justified.
- (Ad hoc hypothesis) Do not defend a claim by adding arbitrary assumptions each time a rebuttal appears.

## Circular reasoning

- (Begging the question) Do not put the conclusion into the premises.
- (Tautology) Do not reason as "A, therefore A". Build the grounds from propositions other than the conclusion.
- (Circular definition) Do not include the concept being defined in its definition.
- (Fallacy fallacy) A fallacy in someone's argument does not prove their conclusion false.

## Cognitive biases

- (Confirmation bias) Do not gather only information that fits your view. Seek disconfirming information.
- (Cherry-picking) Do not quote only favorable cases. Review unfavorable cases too.
- (Availability bias) Do not mistake easily recalled cases for frequent ones.
- (Representativeness bias) Do not judge the whole by a few salient features.
- (Survivorship bias) Do not infer success factors from successful cases alone. Analyze failures as well.
- (Halo effect) Do not extend excellence in one area to unrelated areas.
- (Anchoring bias) Do not rely too heavily on the first information you encountered.
- (Proximity bias) Do not rate nearby subjects more favorably.
- (Backfire effect) Do not harden existing beliefs when you meet disconfirming information. Stay open to revising beliefs.
- (Selection bias) Check that no group is over- or under-represented in sampling.
- (Out-group homogeneity bias) Do not recognize diversity in your own group while ignoring it in other groups.
- (Rosy retrospection) Do not glorify the past or belittle the present. Compare time points objectively.

## Burden of proof and verification standards

- The side making a claim carries the burden of proof. Do not demand proof of absence from the side that rebuts.
- A hypothesis becomes testable only when it is falsifiable.
- Verify the formal validity of a proposition and the truth of its premises separately.
- For statistical inference, state the sample size, sampling method, and representativeness.
- Distinguish correlation from causation. A high correlation coefficient does not prove causation.
- Do not raise a single case to a general law. Present several cases or an explanation of the mechanism.
- When you cite an authority, check that its field of expertise matches the field of the statement.

## Exposure rules

- Do not use framework terms such as "So What", "Why So", "MECE", fallacy names, or bias names in the output.
- Do not declare self-application, as in "classified by the MECE principle" or "checked for appeal to authority, so the claim is valid".
- Do not use paraphrases that hint at the terms without naming them, as in "each item operates on one dimension with no overlap between items".
- Do not justify yourself, as in "this classification covers everything without omission" or "each branch is mutually exclusive". Justifying the output by "it followed the principles, so it is valid" is itself an appeal to authority.
- Do not add self-check sections the user did not request, such as "Limitations of the analysis", "Notes for review", "Methodology", "Structure explanation", or "Analytical framework". Evaluate the logic of your own output only when the user explicitly asks.
- Do not expand the work beyond the scope of the request on your own.
- When asked to review the logical structure of material under analysis, do so. Pointing out logical fallacies or cognitive biases in that material within the body is fine.
- Cite external principles unrelated to this file as usual (for example, "prepared under accounting standards").

## Checklist

- Is the conclusion stated?
- Does each conclusion have supporting evidence?
- Can the causal or implicational link of the evidence be verified?
- Do classification items avoid overlap and cover the whole without gaps?
- Is each word used in only one sense?
- Did you avoid asserting causation from temporal sequence?
- Did you avoid raising a special case to a general law?
- Did you review opposing views and counterexamples?
- Does the side making the claim carry the burden of proof?
- Does the conclusion stay within the scope of the evidence?
- Is each cited authority an expert in the relevant field?
- Is there one classification criterion?
- Can conclusion and evidence be traced in both directions?
- Are claims framed as falsifiable propositions?
