# Communication-Oriented Writing

> This skill is an English translation of the original Korean version. The translation may make the skill behave differently from what the original intends.

Write text that speaks directly to a reader in polite prose. Put the main point the reader needs near the top. Give each sentence one idea the reader needs.

## Terms

- (Reader) The person who will read the text and reply, act, or learn something new. There can be more than one.
- (Purpose) What the reader wants to achieve by reading the text, such as reporting, deciding, arranging a schedule, or solving a problem.
- (Main point) The one thing the reader needs to know first from the text. It is an answer, a request, a change, or a judgment.
- (Opener) A text in which the writer starts the conversation. Requests, announcements, and proposals are openers.
- (Reply) A text that responds to a message or a piece of work received from the reader. Answers to inquiries, feedback, responses to complaints, disagreements, and refusals are replies.
- (Idea) One fact, judgment, or request for the reader to take in.
- (Grounds) The facts, data, or observations that show a claim or judgment is right.
- (Greeting) The opening greeting, form of address, self-introduction, closing line, and signature. Leave greetings, subject lines, and titles out when you count sentences and paragraphs.

## Scope

Apply this skill to text written for a reader that tells, asks, or requests something of that reader.

- (Work contact) emails, messenger texts, collaboration requests, shared meeting agendas
- (Responses) replies to customer inquiries and complaints, feedback on work, comments on issues and code reviews
- (Notices) announcements, guides, release notes, README descriptions, public relations copy
- (Public posts) community posts and replies, blog posts, essays, newsletters, social media posts

## Register

The default register is polite. In Korean, the default polite style is 합니다체, with sentences ending in -습니다 or -합니다. On a messenger, when a reader whose rank and seniority are both the same as or lower than yours writes in 해요체 (the softer polite style ending in -요), reply in 해요체. Customer-facing text keeps the default polite register even on a messenger (in Korean, 합니다체).

When the user asks for plain style (Korean 평서체, endings -이다 and -한다), write that one text in plain declarative sentences. Plain style follows the formal written declarative style of blogs and technical documents. Convert the request and suggestion forms below into plain declarative forms as well, such as "it would help if you did X" or "you can do X" (in Korean, "-해 주면 좋겠다" or "-하면 된다").

A request or a suggestion states the action the reader is to take. Set the strength of the wording by whom the action helps and by your relationship with the reader.

- (Request) A sentence that asks the reader to do something the writer needs. When you first ask something of a reader at an outside organization or another company, ask whether it is possible, as in "Would it be possible to send it by the 30th?" When you request data or access from such a reader, also ask them to tell you if a specific department or application process handles such requests. Ask other readers directly, as in "Could you send it by Thursday?" or "I would appreciate your decision by Friday." When an announcement tells many readers what to do, write "Please ...". If the reader must do it by role, contract, or rule, name that role, contract, or rule once in the body. Use "I would appreciate ..." or "I would be grateful ..." only once per text in total (in Korean, "부탁드립니다" and "감사하겠습니다"). This phrase does not count toward the thanks limit.
- (Suggestion) A sentence that tells the reader a method that helps them. Write it as an action the reader can take, as in "You can fix this by renaming the key in the config file."

## Order

Put the main point within the first three body sentences, not counting greetings, subject lines, or titles.

### Openers

Place at most one sentence before the main point. If the main point does not make sense on its own, put one sentence of background before it. If no background is needed, one sentence of thanks or apology may go before it.

Write a request with the request and its deadline first, then the reason, then the information the reader needs to decide, such as format or alternatives. At the end, add the deliverable the reader will receive or what changes for the reader after they answer. Write an announcement with the change first, then who it affects and when, then what the reader must do, then the reason for the change.

### Replies

Write a reply in the order below. This order adapts RASA (Receive, Appreciate, Summarize, Ask), a model of listening, to writing.

1. (Receive) Before writing, read the whole message. Find the action the reader requested, the facts the reader reported, and any feeling the reader stated in words. Also find the purpose the reader wants to achieve with the requested action. If the message does not show a purpose, treat the requested action as the purpose.
2. (Acknowledge) If the reader reported a problem, gave an opinion, or made a proposal, write an acknowledgment sentence that takes up the facts the reader wrote. If work the reader sent for review has something done well, state one such thing specifically, as a fact. If the reader stated a feeling, acknowledge the fact that caused it in place of the feeling word. If you have not yet confirmed the reported problem, acknowledge it with a sentence of thanks for the report. If the reader only did something you asked for, acknowledge it with a sentence of thanks for that task. If the reader gave a circumstance as the reason for a request, treat that circumstance as a problem the reader reported.
3. (Summarize) Name the reader's request or claim inside the main-point sentence, as in "I'm sorry, but a one-week extension will not work because the final report is due on May 30." If there are several requests, name each one and answer them in turn. If a request can be read in two or more ways, list the possible readings inside the question.
4. (Answer) Choose as the main point an answer that lets the reader decide their next step toward their purpose. Give the main point its grounds. Place the acknowledgment and the apology before the main point, at most one sentence each. If the reader asked for information, put the main point in the first sentence and place the acknowledgment and apology after it. Treat a question that asks for an action, such as "Could you ...?", as a request. If the acknowledgment and the apology cover the same fact, merge them into one sentence. When the reply disagrees or declines, offer an alternative if one exists. State who does what in the alternative. Write the writer's part as "I will ..." or "We will ...". Ask the reader for any step that needs their answer.
5. (Ask) If the answer needs information that the message does not contain, ask for it. Ask only for what you do not know. Keep premises that steer the answer out of the question. Even when there are several questions, keep them in one text so the reader can answer them in a single response. If you can give part of the answer, give it first and put each question after the answer it relates to. If you cannot answer at all, put the question in the main-point position.

### Greetings and subject lines

Start every email, and every messenger text to someone you are contacting for the first time, with a one-line opening greeting. Start a messenger text to someone you already know with a form of address such as "Hi [Name],". Add a self-introduction to the opening greeting when the reader does not know the writer. End an email with a closing line and a signature. In a first email to someone at an outside organization or another company, put your organization and contact details in the signature. A closing line such as "Thank you," (in Korean, "감사합니다.") does not count toward the thanks limit. In an opener email or a post, write the main point as a noun phrase in the subject line or title. Start any customer-facing text with a one-line opening greeting, whatever the channel. Start other comments, board replies, board announcements, and public posts directly with the body.

## Sentences

### Prose

Write every sentence as a complete sentence with a subject and a verb (in Korean, a sentence that ends with its predicate).

Use bullets or a numbered list when there are three or more parallel items that each take a sentence or more, or when there are steps the reader follows in order. Write complete sentences inside bullets too.

### One idea per sentence

Put one idea in each sentence. When a connective such as "and", "but", or "while" (Korean connective endings such as -고, -며, -지만, -는데) joins two ideas, split them into two sentences. A condition ("if" or Korean -하면) or a reason ("because" or Korean -해서) belongs to the idea it modifies. Keep a condition or a reason in the same sentence as its idea.

### Noun strings

When three or more space-separated nouns come in a row, the reader has to guess how the nouns relate. When you have built such a phrase yourself, remove the nouns that are not essential or open up the phrase with prepositions, articles, or verbs so the relationships show. Rewrite "login path test addition request" as "a request to add tests for the login path." Keep subject lines, titles, proper names, terms the reader used first, and terms common in the reader's line of work or in the channel as they are. Proper names are the names of organizations, products, documents, programs, projects, and system items. Date and time expressions, numbers, and the units attached to numbers (such as "Q3") do not count as nouns.

### Punctuation

Punctuation marks and symbols are part of the message the reader reads. Use a mark only where deleting it would change the meaning or the places where the reader pauses. Keep quoted text and labels placed before a sentence, such as "Subject:" or "(Background)", as they are.

- (Commas) Use commas between three or more parallel items and where a pause makes the meaning clear, as after a form of address, after an opening phrase or clause, or in "I'm sorry, but". Join two items in a sentence with "and" or "or".
- (Parentheses) Use parentheses for short supplements such as the expansion of an abbreviation, the original-language form of a term, or a unit or format. Write anything the reader needs in order to act or decide in a sentence outside the parentheses.
- (Quotation marks) Use quotation marks for text you copy or the reader must find letter for letter, such as a quote from the received message or a menu name on screen.
- (Question marks) Use a question mark for a question you want the reader to answer.
- (Exclamation marks) Avoid exclamation marks where possible. Show emphasis through the position of the main point and specific wording.
- (Other symbols) Write out in words the colons, slashes, arrows, hyphens, and similar symbols that stand for a relationship or range inside a sentence. Keep times such as "2:00 p.m." and hyphenated words such as "two-step" as they are.

### Specificity

- Write deadlines and schedules with at least one of a weekday, a date, or a time, as in "by 3 p.m. this Friday."
- If the reader's task has a deadline, state the action and the deadline together.
- When you ask the reader for information, a decision, or approval, state the deliverable the reader will receive or what changes for the reader once they answer, if there is one.
- If there are several problems, state each one specifically, one at a time.
- When you ask for a review, state the items to review and the result you want.
- When you describe an improvement, state the current state and the state after the change.
- Call things by their specific names in place of vague nouns such as "thing", "part", "point", or "aspect" (in Korean, 것, 부분, 점, 곳, 셈, 편). Keep fixed expressions (Korean "먹을 것" for "something to eat"), grammatical words (Korean "-할 수 있다" for "can"), and counters (Korean "5곳" for "five places") as they are.

### Terminology

Use terms the reader used first as they are. Use terms common in the reader's line of work or in the channel as they are. Explain any other abbreviation, industry term, or internal term once, where it first appears.

## Attitude

### Acknowledgment

An acknowledgment of a problem, opinion, or proposal takes up a fact the reader reported and adds the writer's confirmation, agreement, thanks, or apology. Write it like "We can reproduce the save error you described under the same conditions." An acknowledgment of work states what was done well and its grounds, as in "I could check the Chapter 3 estimate right away because it shows its sources and formulas."

### Courtesy phrases

- Thank the reader once when the reader did something for the writer or made the writer a proposal. Name that task in the sentence, as in "Thank you for sending the raw data."
- Apologize once when the writer's side caused the reader trouble. State what went wrong in the apology sentence. If several things went wrong, list them side by side in the one apology sentence and count that sentence as one idea. A text with an apology also states the fix or the schedule. State the cause of the mistake in one sentence after the fix or the schedule. When you decline the reader's request and your side is not at fault, open the refusal sentence with "I'm sorry, but" (in Korean, "죄송하지만"). This phrase does not count as an apology sentence.

### Criticism

- Make the problem the subject. Write "This table is missing the base year."
- After naming the problem, state the fix or an alternative as an action the reader can take. Write the fix as an action that removes the cause of the problem.
- If the reader cannot tell why a problem needs fixing from the problem alone, add one sentence on how it affects the reader's purpose.
- Rewrite a prohibition such as "You must not do X" as a sentence with the action and its effect, such as "If you check the format before saving, the file will save without errors."
- For wrong content, place the written value and the correct value side by side so the difference shows.

## Logic

### Grounds

- Make only claims you can give grounds for. Give each claim its grounds, as a reason in the same sentence or in one or two sentences after it.
- Take the facts, dates, and figures about the case at hand from the information the user gave and the received message. If you need more facts from the writer's side, ask the user. If the reader's task needs a submission address, link, or date that the given information lacks, mark the spot with a placeholder such as [link] and tell the user what to fill in.
- Write opinions as the writer's judgment, such as "I think X" or "I propose X." State the source of each fact. For what happened on the writer's side, cite a source only when the reader can look it up in a document.
- When you ask the reader to decide, state for each option how its cost, schedule, and result differ. If the writer recommends an option, name it and give the reason. If the decision has a deadline, state it.

### Consistency

Within the same context, keep definitions, scope, and criteria as you first set them.

- (Definition) Use each word with one meaning. Each time you refer to the same thing, use the name you first gave it.
- (Scope) Choose the topics you cover by what the reader needs for their purpose. When you widen or narrow the scope, state the new scope.
- (Criteria) When you compare several items, apply the same criteria to every item. When you change a criterion for judgment, state the new criterion and the reason for the change.

### Conclusions

If the reader asked for a judgment, reach a conclusion on one side. Decide the direction by the quality of the grounds. Give more weight to grounds that rest on confirmed facts and to grounds that apply directly to the case at hand. If something is uncertain, state what is uncertain. In that case, also state what needs to be checked before you can conclude. When the grounds favor neither side and you postpone a judgment, state the condition under which you will conclude.

### Reader's view

Center the text on what the reader has to do and what changes for the reader. Describe benefits and good news as results the reader gets, as in "With this feature, you can cut repeated data entry." Pass on the outcome of internal review as results that affect the reader.

## Expressions to avoid

Apart from greetings and sentences of acknowledgment, thanks, or apology, do not write a sentence whose deletion leaves the reader with the same information. The expressions below are typical examples.

- (Cushions) "I was wondering if," "If it's not too much trouble," "I know you're busy, but" (in Korean, "다름이 아니라", "혹시 실례가 되지 않는다면", "바쁘신 줄 알지만")
- (Self-lowering) expressions that lower the writer, such as "humble as I am" or "if I may be so bold" (in Korean, "부족한 제가" or "감히")
- (Evaluative praise) words that judge the reader or the work without content, such as "Great question," "Excellent point," "That's truly amazing," or "Perfect." Such words put the writer in the position of judging the reader.
- (Exclamatory courtesy) thanks and apologies inflated with exclamation, such as "Thank you so much"
- (Goodwill fillers) sentences that carry no information and only maintain goodwill, such as "I hope this helps" or "Feel free to reach out"
- (Echoes) sentences that copy the received message or restate it, as in "I understand this as a request to X"
- (Text commentary) sentences that preview the structure of the text or why it was written, such as "I'll explain in detail below," "First I'll cover A," or "In response to your request"
- (Process reports) phrases that report the writer's own work, such as "After reviewing it" or "Having checked"
- (Tool exposure) names of frameworks, methods, or skills used to write the text
- (Empty connectors) "In this context," "This has significant implications," "It is worth noting"
- (Hype words) "groundbreaking," "innovative," "game-changer," "paradigm shift"
- (Broad nouns) "ecosystem," "discourse," "landscape" used without a specific referent
- (Formula rhetoric) contrast that negates a phrase for emphasis ("not A, but a true B"), asking and answering your own question, and announcing a count before the content ("three key strategies")
- (Translationese in Korean text) Change "-에 있어서" to "-에서". Change "-하지 않으면 안 된다" to "-해야 한다". Remove "-라고 하는" by reordering the sentence. Do not stack the suffixes -적 and -화.
- (Repetition) sentences that repeat the same content in other words and sentences that re-summarize the body at the end
- (Symbols) em dashes, ellipses for dramatic effect, bold for emphasis inside sentences, emojis, exclamation marks, tildes, chat abbreviations such as "ㅎㅎ" or "lol"

## Rhythm

- When three sentences in a row have similar length, add a condition or a reason to one of them to change its length. Within one paragraph, treat three consecutive sentences as similar in length when the longest and the shortest differ by one space-separated word or less (in Korean, one 어절).
- Write one topic per paragraph in two to five sentences. Use a one-sentence paragraph only as the first paragraph. From the second paragraph on, attach content that takes only one sentence to the paragraph whose topic is closest to it. A sentence attached this way does not count as a topic of that paragraph.
- When two sentences in a row share the same subject-verb frame (in Korean, the same subject particle and the same predicate form, such as '-입니다', '-합니다', '-있습니다'), give the third sentence a different frame. After "A is B. C is D.", write a sentence with a different frame, such as "E has F."
- Use the same predicate expression, such as "You can ..." or "Could you ...?" (in Korean, "-하면 됩니다" or "-해 주실 수 있을까요?"), at most twice in one text.

## Examples

### Request (email)

(before)

```
I was wondering if I could ask you a small favor when you have a moment?
I know you're busy, but if at all possible...
```

(after)

```
Subject: Request for raw data from the Q3 satisfaction survey by this Thursday

Hi [Name], I'm [Your name] from the research and analysis team.

Could you send the raw data from the Q3 satisfaction survey by this Thursday? We need to re-tabulate the results by region for next Monday's report. We need the raw data because the summary table has no regional breakdown. Raw data with the personal information columns removed will still work for the tabulation. Once we receive the raw data, we will share a draft of the regional tables by Friday afternoon this week.

Thank you,
[Your name]
```

### Announcement (internal board)

(before)

```
Hello, we have an important notice to share.
To start with some background, we recently reviewed various security matters from multiple angles, and...
```

(after)

```
Title: Two-step verification for the shared drive from October 15

From October 15, access to the shared drive will require two-step verification.

Two-step verification applies to every account, including those of external partners. Please register an authenticator app in your account settings by October 14. We decided on two-step verification following a recommendation from last month's security audit.
```

### Answer to an inquiry (inquiry board reply)

(Received) "The survey results file you shared won't open. This is my second inquiry, after yesterday."

(before)

```
Hello! Thank you so much for contacting us. I'll now respond to the issue you mentioned.
We sincerely apologize for the inconvenience. There could be many causes for this problem...
```

(after)

```
Hello [Name],

We're sorry for sharing a file that won't open and for not giving you a fix when you first contacted us yesterday. By 3 p.m. today, we will upload a copy converted to the previous format at the same link.

After we changed the file's save format yesterday evening, the file stopped opening in older versions of the program. If the copy converted to the previous format still won't open, we will check it right away once you tell us your program's name and version.
```

### Feedback (messenger)

(Received) "Here's the draft of the market analysis report. Could you take a look?"

(before)

```
What a great report! I'll mention a few minor things.
The first thing I want to bring up is about the tables...
```

(after)

```
Hi [Name], I could check the market size you estimated in Chapter 3 right away because you included the sources and formulas.

The table comparing competitors in Chapter 4 is missing the base year. You can fix this by adding the base year to the table title. Without the base year, readers might compare the figures with numbers from a different year.

The growth forecast in the Chapter 5 conclusion has no source. If you add sources the same way as in Chapter 3, the grounds will carry through to the conclusion.
```

### Disagreement (email)

(Received) "To make the results more representative, I think we should expand the survey nationwide."

(before)

```
What you said makes some sense, but from various perspectives
there are aspects where it isn't necessarily so. There may be different opinions, but...
```

(after)

```
Hi [Name],

I agree that the results need to be more representative. I propose that we enlarge the capital-region sample for this survey in place of a nationwide expansion.

Nationwide responses would be hard to use in the conclusions because the terms of reference set the project scope to capital-region policy. If we spend the same budget on the capital-region sample, I think the error margin for each area will shrink because more responses will be usable in the conclusions. If a nationwide comparison is needed, we will attach public national statistics as reference material.

Thank you,
[Your name]
```

## Checklist

- Is the main point within the first three body sentences, not counting greetings, subject lines, or titles?
- Apart from greetings, acknowledgments, thanks, and apologies, is there no sentence whose deletion leaves the reader with the same information?
- Is the reader's task written as an action, with a weekday, date, or time when it has a deadline?
- Does every claim have grounds, and does every fact about the case come from the information you were given?
- Does each sentence hold one idea?
- Do thanks and apology each appear at most once, with their object stated?
- In a reply, can the reader decide their next step from the main point alone?
- In a reply, did you use acknowledgment and questions only when their conditions apply?
- Does each criticism make the problem the subject and state the fix?
- Within the same context, did you refer to the same thing by the name you first gave it and apply the same criteria to every item you compared?
- Did you explain, where they first appear, terms the reader did not use and that are not common in the reader's work or channel?
- Apart from the names and terms kept as they are, did you open up every run of three or more space-separated nouns?
- Would deleting each punctuation mark change the meaning or the places where the reader pauses, and are two items in a sentence joined with "and" or "or" in place of a comma?
- Is every expression from the list of expressions to avoid gone?
- Is there no run of three or more sentences with the same subject-verb frame, and does no predicate expression appear three or more times in one text?
- If it is an email, does it have an opening greeting, a closing line, and a signature, and does an opener email have a subject line?

## Exceptions

- When the user specifies a register or tone, follow that instruction.
- When the user asks for emojis or a casual tone, use them.
- When you quote the reader's original text, copy it exactly.
- For texts with a fixed format, such as commit messages and press releases, follow that format first.
- For contracts and legal documents, follow the conventions of that document type.
- Do not apply the rules of this skill to content inside code blocks.
